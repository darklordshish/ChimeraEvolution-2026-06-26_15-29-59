## 1. Which features drive animal category recognition from outlines: global proportions vs local details

### Takeaway
Silhouettes alone carry enough information for human object and animal identification, and the evidence says that **global part structure (the skeleton: how many limbs/parts, where they attach, and their relative sizes)** drives coarse category ("animal", "quadruped") recognition. **Local diagnostic parts** (snout, ears, horns, tail) then separate one species from another. The two work in order, coarse first and details second. A hybrid that changes only local details while keeping the base skeleton and proportions will read as "base animal wearing accessories". That is exactly the "glued parts" symptom.

### Cited Findings
- Silhouettes of everyday objects were identified correctly 64.7% of the time on average, outlines 61.85%. Identifiability per item ranged from 0% to 100%, so some shapes carry their identity in the outline and some do not. **[STRONG, large norming study]** — [Wagemans et al. 2008, Perception](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1068/p5825); summary figures via [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Identification-of-Everyday-Objects-on-the-Basis-of-Wagemans-Winter/b7cfaaaac3203c0e1e2f2a1ec0351a8790a033ba)
- Human superordinate classification of natural contours (animal vs leaf) is "well understood as" a simple statistical (Bayesian) classification of the **shape skeleton** (medial axis), tuned to natural shape statistics. In other words, the branching part structure is what makes an outline read as an animal. **[MODERATE-STRONG]** — [Wilder, Feldman & Singh 2011, Cognition 119:325–340](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0010027711000357) (abstract-level summary via search; [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21440250/))
- "Shape skeletons modulate perceived similarity": skeletal structure affects human similarity judgments beyond contour overlap. **[MODERATE]** — [Lowet, Firestone & Scholl 2018 (JHU Perception Lab)](https://perception.jhu.edu/files/PDFs/18_ShapeSkeletons_Similarity/Lowet-EtAl-SkeletalSimilarity.pdf) (title/claim only; full text blocked, 403)
- Recognition-by-Components: objects are recognized from a small set of volumetric parts (geons) and their relations (relative size, attachment position). The theory handles rigid objects well. Patients with left-hemisphere damage were specifically impaired on **contorted poses of nonrigid objects (animals)**, which shows that animals are harder to capture by parts alone and that pose/articulation matters. **[MODERATE; theory contested]** — [Wikipedia: Recognition-by-components](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recognition-by-components_theory); [Biederman 1987 PDF](https://cim.mcgill.ca/~siddiqi/COMP-558-2012/rbc.pdf)
- Research on animal recognition in natural images reports that luminance and colour have less influence on recognition than texture, shape and certain **diagnostic parts** (e.g. eyes and mouth). **[MODERATE]** — [JOV, Animal detection and identification in natural scenes](https://jov.arvojournals.org/article.aspx?articleid=2192082) (via search summary)
- A database of more than 25,000 animal silhouettes, with many shape descriptors combined into one metric, produced a "shape space" with prominent shape-based organization. Nearby silhouettes share shape characteristics, and a combined-descriptor model predicted human shape similarity. **[MODERATE]** — [Morgenstern, Schmidt & Fleming, ShapeComp (bioRxiv)](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2020.01.10.901876.full.pdf) (search summary; PDF too large to fetch)
- Valve validated TF2 character designs in the concept phase by checking that the characters could be identified **from silhouette alone with no internal shading**. Silhouettes are named as critical for long-range readability and for readability under different lighting. **[MODERATE, industry paper]** — [Mitchell & Francke, Illustrative Rendering in Team Fortress 2, NPAR 2007](https://steamcdn-a.akamaihd.net/apps/valve/2007/NPAR07_IllustrativeRenderingInTeamFortress2.pdf); [Coelho-Kostolny on TF2/Overwatch readability](https://medium.com/@xavierck/character-readability-in-team-fortress-2-and-overwatch-68c41d454465)
- Deep CNNs "do not classify based on global object shape". By this paper's title and thesis, CNNs lean on local contour features where humans use global shape. **[MODERATE]** — [Baker, Lu, Erlikhman & Kellman 2018, PLoS Comput Biol (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6306249/) (full text captcha-blocked; only title/thesis verified)

### Inferences
- **Priority for fog at 20 m** (where fine detail such as ear tips, snout profile and horn tines is lost to blur and contrast loss): (1) **posture and stance**, i.e. horizontal quadruped vs upright biped, hunch, head carriage; (2) **proportions of the large masses**, i.e. head:torso, neck length, limb length vs torso, fore/hind balance, tail length; (3) **big protruding diagnostic parts**, i.e. muzzle length, ears as spikes, antlers as a wide crown, spines as a fuzzy halo. Small details only matter at close range. [WEAK→MODERATE, inference from the skeleton-classification and silhouette-norming results]
- "Human + wolf" reads only if the **skeleton-level silhouette** moves toward wolf: a forward-thrust head on a more horizontal neck, a long muzzle, a deeper chest with a narrower waist, hunched shoulders, digitigrade-looking hind legs, and a tail. A wolf head glued on an upright human torso is read as "human in a mask". This matches the reported symptom.
- Each donor has 1–2 **"signature" silhouette elements** that survive blur and should be weighted most heavily: wolf = long muzzle + upright pointed ears + tail + deep chest/tucked belly; moose = shoulder hump + very long legs + palmate antler crown + long overhanging muzzle; hedgehog = round spiny halo, short legs hidden, pointed snout; snake = limbless elongation, no limb protrusions; owl = round head merged into body (no neck), ear tufts, wide wing mass. (Species characterisations are my own anatomical inference, not sourced.)

### Gaps
- No study found that directly measures **which animal parts** carry recognition in silhouettes with part-by-part ablation, e.g. "remove the tail and how much wolf-ness is lost". Such data would give direct weights. A cheap in-house test could supply it.
- No perception data found for **fog/distance-degraded silhouettes of animals** specifically. That is effectively a low-pass-filtered silhouette, which argues for weighting coarse structure more heavily.

## 2. Caricature/exaggeration: does going past 50/50 (exaggerating distinctive features) improve recognition? Implications for blend weights

### Takeaway
There are two robust effects. (a) **Morph continua are perceived categorically**: identity flips sharply near a boundary, not linearly, so a linear 50/50 mix sits right on the ambiguous boundary and reads as neither. (b) **Caricature**, meaning exaggeration of deviations from the norm, improves recognition. The optimal exaggeration is modest for realistic images (~4–6%) and larger for simplified line drawings (~16–42%). With noisy or error-laden depictions, exaggeration also magnifies errors. For a stylized low-poly game, both argue for **nonlinear, per-region blend weights**: per region, commit clearly to one identity (weight pushed away from 0.5), and exaggerate the donor's distinctive deviation somewhat beyond the veridical donor value where the donor dominates.

### Cited Findings
- **Categorical perception across morphs** (faces): in a linear morph between two familiar faces, discrimination is most accurate for pairs that straddle the apparent category boundary. Individual identities are perceived categorically, not linearly. **[STRONG, widely replicated]** — [Beale & Keil 1995, Cognition](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8556842/)
- **Categorical boundary for animal-shape morphs**: monkeys categorized 3D morphs that were linear combinations of 3 prototype cats and 3 prototype dogs. Near-boundary stimuli on opposite sides look similar yet are categorized differently, and prefrontal neurons encoded the category, not the raw shape. **[STRONG]** — [Freedman, Riesenhuber, Poggio & Miller 2001, Science 291:312](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11209083/)
- **Caricature advantage**: caricatures are recognized better than veridical faces, and true caricatures of previously seen faces beat the originals. Explanation: faces are coded as deviations from a prototype, and exaggeration amplifies the encoded deviations. **[STRONG]** — [Rhodes/Caricature and face recognition, Memory & Cognition 1992](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03210927)
- **Optimal exaggeration levels** (review in Frowd et al.): Rhodes, Brennan & Carey (1987), line drawings at 0 to ±75% in 25% steps → best likeness ≈ **+16%**, with 50% caricature named faster than veridical and veridical faster than 50% anti-caricature. Benson & Perrett (1994), line drawings of famous faces → best likeness ≈ **+42%**. Photographs: Benson & Perrett (1991) ≈ **+4%**, Ellis (1990) ≈ **+6%**. Lee & Perrett showed a +50% caricature advantage when images were flashed briefly (67 ms), i.e. under degraded viewing conditions. **[STRONG]** — [Frowd, Bruce, Ross, McIntyre & Hancock, "An application of caricature: how to improve the recognition of facial composites" (Stirling)](https://www.stir.ac.uk/media/stirling/services/faculties/natural-sciences/documents/caricature-paper.pdf)
- **Caveat, caricature exaggerates errors too**: for error-laden facial composites, participants preferred an **anti-caricature** (mean −14.7%; sketches −33.1%). For morphed (averaged, lower-error) composites they preferred **+7.2%**. The authors conclude that exaggeration also amplifies whatever is wrong with the depiction. **[MODERATE]** — [Frowd et al. (Stirling)](https://www.stir.ac.uk/media/stirling/services/faculties/natural-sciences/documents/caricature-paper.pdf)
- **Norm-based (opponent) coding**: identity is coded as the direction and distance from an average "norm" in face space. Adapting to the "anti-face" (the opposite identity) facilitates recognition along the same identity trajectory. So the *direction* from the norm defines identity, and *distance* along it defines identity strength. **[STRONG]** — [Rhodes & Leopold / Adaptive norm-based coding of facial identity, Vision Research 2006](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S004269890600143X); [Rhodes 2017, Current Directions](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0963721417692786)
- Reverse caricature effect: familiarization with caricatures improves later recognition of veridical faces. **[MODERATE]** — [PubMed 21132058](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21132058)

### Inferences (for a blend-weight model)
- **Model each region's proportion as a point in a "species space" with a norm.** The norm can be a generic mammal average or the chassis species. Species identity is the *direction* of a region's deviation from the norm. Blending then means moving along that direction, and caricature means moving past the donor value along the same direction.
- **Avoid linear 50/50 per region.** Because perception is categorical, a region at t=0.5 is maximally ambiguous. Use a steep sigmoid or a quantized weight per region, e.g. donor share 0.3 → region weight ~0.15, share 0.5 → 0.6, share 0.7 → 0.9. **Different regions should flip at different thresholds**, e.g. head flips first, then limbs, then torso. The whole body then shows a readable mosaic of committed regions ("human torso, wolf head and legs") rather than a uniformly ambiguous average. [MODERATE inference from categorical perception]
- **Caricature factor for stylized silhouettes**: the game is low-poly and stylized (closer to line drawings than photos), and viewing in fog is degraded (like the 67 ms flash). The literature therefore supports exaggerating the donor's **distinctive** deviations by roughly **+15% to +40%** beyond the veridical donor ratio in regions the donor owns. [MODERATE inference]
- **But**: exaggeration amplifies errors. If the region shape is wrong (bad joint, clipping, a proportion the base skeleton can't carry), caricaturing it makes it worse (Frowd et al.). Fix geometric correctness before adding exaggeration. [MODERATE]
- Exaggerate only **distinctive** dimensions, i.e. those where the donor deviates most from the norm (wolf: muzzle length, ear height, tail; moose: leg length, shoulder hump, antler span). Leave dimensions where the donor is near-average unexaggerated. That is exactly what a caricature generator does.

### Gaps
- No caricature/morph studies found on **whole-body animal silhouettes or human–animal hybrids** specifically. The quantitative optima come from faces. Transfer to body silhouettes is plausible (the same norm-based coding is proposed for bodies and objects) but unverified.
- No data on where the categorical boundary lies for human↔animal body morphs, i.e. which share makes a figure "read as wolf-man" vs "man". This would need an in-house test.

## 3. Creature-design practice for hybrids: dominant vs secondary, which traits to borrow, avoiding the "glued parts" look, werewolf specifics

### Takeaway
Practitioners converge on the same rules. **One animal owns the skeleton and locomotion**, and the second contributes a limited set of features. Integration comes from making the **transition zones and the line of action/spine** continuous, and from functional plausibility (joints, muscle mass where the creature acts). Repeating shapes or curves across the body makes it read as one organism. For werewolves, the key decisions are digitigrade vs plantigrade legs, head-to-body ratio, where the shoulders sit, and hands/claws. The most wolf-reading designs push the human body toward a wolf's posture (forward-set shoulders, digitigrade hands and feet). Evidence here is practitioner opinion, not experiment.

### Cited Findings
- Guidance to "anchor the creature to one species' skeleton so it reads as a single organism instead of two animals pasted together". Heuristic split: **70% primary base = locomotion identity**, **20% secondary = a feature twist** (ears, arms, feet), **10% hook = one iconic detail**. "Too many influences result in incoherent design." **[WEAK, tutorial]** — [PixelSanctuary, Creature Design Anatomy: Hybrid Reference](https://www.pixelsanctuary.com/tutorials/creature-design-anatomy-hybrid-reference)
- The same source's anti-"glued" advice: joint placement that enables a realistic range of motion; muscle mass concentrated where the creature acts; anchor five structural points (spine curve, rib cage, pelvis, shoulder/hip joints, hands/feet); connect volumes with intentional curves; treat the spine as the line of action. It also recommends **repetition** (echo the tail shape in the ears, reuse the same curve in the back and horns) so the design feels intentional. **[WEAK]** — [PixelSanctuary](https://www.pixelsanctuary.com/tutorials/creature-design-anatomy-hybrid-reference); repetition point via search summary of the same page
- Whitlatch (Star Wars creature designer) builds chimaeras from named donor animals. Her course has students mix a canine or feline base with another animal, i.e. a fixed dominant base. **[MODERATE, professional practice]** — [Planet Furaha review](http://planetfuraha.blogspot.com/2016/05/terryl-whitlatchs-creature-design.html); [Gnomon workshop](https://thegnomonworkshop.com/workshops/creature-design-with-terryl-whitlatch)
- A critical review of Whitlatch notes that some chimaeras are visually striking but **functionally unintegrated** (a dog-fish "does not make much sense as an animal"). Her multi-leg designs just duplicate front legs close together. This is the "glued" failure mode even among professionals. **[MODERATE, expert critique]** — [Planet Furaha](http://planetfuraha.blogspot.com/2016/05/terryl-whitlatchs-creature-design.html)
- Neville Page (Cloverfield, Avatar, Star Trek) grounds designs in biological plausibility and seeks "that perfect balance in silhouette that sums up the quick read". Example: Cloverfield's proportions evoke a newborn deer or horse (long thin limbs, awkward stance), so a borrowed **proportion scheme** alone carries the animal association. **[MODERATE, professional interview]** — [ArtStation Magazine interview](https://magazine.artstation.com/2018/02/neville-page/); [io9 interview](https://gizmodo.com/io9-talks-to-cloverfield-monster-designer-neville-page-357856)
- Werewolf design: listed key decisions are digitigrade vs plantigrade legs, head-to-body ratio, and hand/claw structure. Artists block out rough silhouettes and **over-exaggerate the silhouette early**, refining later. **[WEAK, workflow blog]** — [Tripo3D werewolf workflow](https://www.tripo3d.ai/blog/explore/werewolf-3d-model)
- One werewolf artist's notes describe a human body "pushed to be almost identical to an ordinary wolf", with **shoulders set toward the front of the torso** and **digitigrade hands and feet**. **[WEAK, artist notes; page itself 403, via search summary]** — [Pandadrake, How I Draw Werewolves](https://www.deviantart.com/pandadrake/art/How-I-Draw-Werewolves-Character-Anatomy-Notes-683178198)
- Hybrid character tips (human-animal) from Clip Studio tutorials exist but were not fetched. **[unverified]** — [Clip Studio: Creating a human-animal hybrid](https://tips.clip-studio.com/en-us/articles/9030)

### Inferences
- The project's "chassis keeps skeleton and size" matches the practitioner "one skeleton owner" rule. It is correct for coherence. It becomes "glued parts" only if the chassis also keeps **all its proportions and posture**. Practitioners borrow a *proportion scheme and posture* from the secondary animal (Page's newborn-deer legs, Pandadrake's forward shoulders), not only its parts.
- **Concrete "human + wolf" shift list** (derived from the above, not tested): head carried forward of the shoulders; neck angled forward, not vertical; shoulders shifted forward and up (hunch); deeper ribcage with narrower waist; lower legs reading digitigrade (a lengthened foot segment with heel raised); hands elongated; tail added. Silhouette-level changes come first, then muzzle and ears.
- **Transition zones** (neck base, shoulder, hip, wrist, ankle) are where "glued" is detected. The chassis mass should *taper into* the donor part's proportions over a zone rather than switching at a seam. In field-based skinning this means blending proportions of neighbouring nodes too, not only the donor's own node.
- **Shape-language repetition across donor parts** (e.g. the wolf's angular ear shape echoed in claws and brow) is a cheap coherence tool. It is a WEAK practitioner claim, but cheap to try.

### Gaps
- No rigorous (experimental) study found of hybrid-creature readability by design choice. All hybrid-design guidance is practitioner opinion.
- Whitlatch's books (*Science of Creature Design*, *Animals Real and Imagined*) were not accessed in full. Her exact rules for choosing which traits to borrow are not documented here.

## 4. Biology: allometry and dimensionless limb ratios by stance type, usable as blendable parameters

### Takeaway
Across mammals from shrew to elephant, limb-bone **lengths scale close to geometric similarity (∝ M^1/3)**. Body *size* therefore barely changes proportions, which justifies keeping chassis size and blending **dimensionless ratios**. Stance type is expressed mainly by the **distal/proximal segment ratio**: plantigrade (human, bear, hedgehog) has short metapodials and a heel on the ground; digitigrade (wolf) has longer metapodials and the ankle raised high; unguligrade (moose) has the longest distal segments. The standard dimensionless ratios (metatarsal/femur, crural index tibia/femur, brachial index radius/humerus, intermembral index) are established, blendable descriptors. Their precise species values were not retrieved.

### Cited Findings
- Lengths and diameters of limb bones from shrews to elephants scale close to geometric similarity: linear dimensions ≈ ∝ mass^1/3. **[STRONG, classic dataset]** — [Alexander, Jayes, Maloiy & Wathuta 1979, J. Zool. 189:305–314 (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/230106352_Allometry_of_the_limb_bones_of_mammals_from_shrews_Sorex_to_elephant_Loxodonta)
- Scaling differs between clades (differential scaling in Carnivora vs other mammals; separate allometry for bovids and for insectivores/rodents). **[STRONG existence, values not retrieved]** — [Bertram & Biewener 1990, J. Morphol.](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/jmor.1052040205); [Allometry of legs of antelopes](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/230216105_Allometry_of_the_legs_of_antelopes_Bovidae); [insectivores & rodents](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/jmor.1051920204)
- Digitigrade and unguligrade mammals typically have **higher metatarsal/femur ratios** than plantigrade ones. Caveat: scaled to body mass, digitigrade carnivores do not have relatively longer metapodials than insectivores/rodents, so the ratio partly reflects proximal-segment differences. **[MODERATE, via search summary]** — [Wikipedia: Digitigrade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digitigrade)
- Digitigrade/unguligrade animals have relatively long carpals/tarsals, with the ankle set much higher in the limb than in humans. Unguligrady gives the greatest effective limb length, with elongated, fused metapodials and reduced digits. Lengthening the distal limb (autopod) relative to the proximal limb (stylopod) increases stride length. **[STRONG, textbook]** — [Animal Diversity Web, Legs, Feet and Cursorial Locomotion](https://animaldiversity.org/collections/mammal_anatomy/running_fast/); [U. Minnesota Vet Anatomy, Plantigrade–Digitigrade–Unguligrade](https://vanat.ahc.umn.edu/run/plate6.html)
- Intermembral index = (humerus + radius)/(femur + tibia) × 100. Below 100 means forelimbs are shorter (leapers, human bipeds), about 100 means quadrupeds, above 100 means brachiators. The crural index (tibia/femur) is one of the standard functional locomotor indices. **[STRONG, standard definitions]** — [Wikipedia: Intermembral index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intermembral_index); [Granatosky 2018, J. Zool.](https://zslpublications.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jzo.12608)
- Multivariate limb-index sets are used to infer locomotor mode from bones. Several ratios together, not one ratio, classify stance and locomotion. **[MODERATE]** — [A multivariate approach to infer locomotor modes in Mesozoic mammals](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/272791355_A_multivariate_approach_to_infer_locomotor_modes_in_Mesozoic_mammals)

### Inferences
- **Blendable parameters, all dimensionless and relative to the chassis's own scale:**
  1. intermembral index (forelimb/hindlimb length): human ~70 (inference; the value is not in the fetched sources, only that bipeds are below 100), quadrupeds ~100;
  2. distal/proximal ratio per limb (metapodial/femur, and foot segment/shank), which encodes stance;
  3. crural and brachial index (tibia/femur, radius/humerus), cursoriality;
  4. limb length / trunk length;
  5. head length / trunk length, and muzzle length / cranium length;
  6. neck length / trunk length, and neck angle to the horizontal;
  7. trunk depth / trunk length, and waist/chest ratio;
  8. tail length / trunk length.

  Species values should be measured from the project's own reference-derived graphs, not taken from literature (none retrieved).
- Since lengths scale ≈ M^1/3, blending ratios while keeping chassis size is biologically consistent. Size does not have to be blended to get "shape of the donor".
- **Stance is a discrete category with a continuous parameter underneath** (heel height / distal ratio). For readability, treat the digitigrade switch like a categorical flip: once the donor's share of the leg crosses a threshold, raise the heel. Interpolating half-way yields an ambiguous "tiptoe human" rather than a readable digitigrade leg. (This follows from section 2.)
- In the source-data sense, "donor data has no meters" fits this: donors should carry ratios like those above, and the chassis applies its own calibre.

### Gaps
- No table of actual numeric ratios (metatarsal/femur etc.) for wolf, moose, human, hedgehog was retrieved. They can be measured from reference images or skeleton plates, or from the literature (e.g. Garland & Janis 1993 limb proportions in mammals; not fetched).
- No source was found that quantifies **how perceptible** each ratio is at distance. The ranking in section 1 is inference.

## 5. Metrics to automatically test "this silhouette looks more like animal X"

### Takeaway
There are classic, cheap, invariant contour metrics: elliptic Fourier descriptors (EFD), the turning-function metric (Arkin et al.), moment invariants, and IoU after normalization. They are easy to implement and are real metrics, but they measure **contour** similarity. Humans weight **skeletal/part structure** and combinations of many descriptors (ShapeComp). CNN embeddings are biased toward local texture/contour rather than global shape. A robust automated "looks more like X" test should therefore combine (a) a skeleton/part-proportion comparison, (b) a low-frequency contour descriptor (first ~10 EFD harmonics, or the turning function on a blurred silhouette to simulate fog), and (c) optionally IoU after pose/scale normalization. Report it as a **relative** score: distance to X vs distance to chassis, and the change in that score with donor share.

### Cited Findings
- **Turning function** (Arkin, Chew, Huttenlocher, Kedem & Mitchell 1991, IEEE PAMI): L2 distance between tangent-angle-vs-arc-length functions of two polygons. It is a true metric, invariant to translation, rotation and scale, O(mn log mn), works for non-convex polygons, and "produces answers that match intuition". A Python implementation exists. **[STRONG, classic]** — [Arkin et al. (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/220181545_An_Efficiently_Computable_Metric_for_Comparing_Polygonal_Shapes); [Python module turning-function](https://github.com/DBraun/turning-function)
- **Elliptic Fourier descriptors**: treat the closed contour as a waveform. They are invariant to rotation, translation and scale after normalization, and Euclidean distance between coefficient vectors is an effective similarity measure. They are widely used in biological morphometrics alongside semi-landmark methods. A fast numpy implementation exists. **[STRONG, standard morphometrics]** — [EFD in 3D contours, C. R. Biologies](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1631069111002940); [Comparison of shape quantification methods (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6872133/); [elliptic-fourier-descriptors (GitHub)](https://github.com/alessandroferrari/elliptic-fourier-descriptors)
- **ShapeComp**: more than 100 descriptors combined over 25,000+ animal silhouettes into a shape space that predicts human shape-similarity judgments. The finding is that no single simple descriptor suffices and combinations do better. **[MODERATE; details not retrieved]** — [Morgenstern, Schmidt & Fleming (bioRxiv)](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2020.01.10.901876.full.pdf)
- **Skeleton-based**: medial-axis (Blum MAT) skeleton statistics predict human animal-vs-leaf classification (Wilder et al.), and skeletons modulate perceived similarity (Lowet et al.). **[MODERATE-STRONG]** — [Wilder et al. 2011](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/50866724_Superordinate_shape_classification_using_natural_shape_statistics); [Lowet et al.](https://perception.jhu.edu/files/PDFs/18_ShapeSkeletons_Similarity/Lowet-EtAl-SkeletalSimilarity.pdf)
- **CNN embeddings**: "Deep convolutional networks do not classify based on global object shape". Their use as a proxy for human global-shape similarity is questionable. Another paper asks whether DNNs are adequate behavioural models of human perception. **[MODERATE]** — [Baker et al. 2018 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6306249/); [arXiv 2305.17023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.17023)

### Inferences (proposed automated test; my design, not sourced)
- **Render** orthographic silhouettes (profile, plus 3/4 and front) of chassis A, donor B and hybrid H. **Blur** them with a Gaussian at the pixel scale of a 20 m foggy view, then threshold. This simulates the coarse channel.
- **Score** s(H) = d(H,A) / (d(H,A)+d(H,B)), where 0 means "looks like chassis" and 1 means "looks like donor". Use d = a weighted sum of (i) the EFD distance on the first ~8–12 harmonics (low-frequency, fog-robust), (ii) the turning-function distance, (iii) 1 − IoU after aligning centroid/principal axis/height, and (iv) the difference in the proportion vector (section 4 ratios measured on the built graph). Items (i)–(iii) are contour metrics; (iv) is the skeleton-level metric the perception literature favours.
- **Guards** that could be written as tests: s(H) is monotonic in donor share; s(H) at a pure-species composition equals 0 or 1; and, following section 2, s(H) should *jump* across a threshold share rather than creep linearly. Record the curve as a detector per chassis×donor pair (the same kind of matrix as an existing chimera matrix).
- Calibrate the weights with a small human test: show blurred silhouettes, ask "wolf / human / wolf-man", and fit weights so s(H) predicts the answers. The literature says the combination must be calibrated, not assumed (ShapeComp).
- Moment invariants (Hu) exist and are cheap, but they are known in practice to be weak discriminators for articulated shapes. I did not find a source for that weakness in this session, so it is WEAK.

### Gaps
- No published direct comparison was found of EFD vs turning function vs IoU **against human judgments on animal silhouettes**. ShapeComp probably contains this, but the details were not retrievable.
- No off-the-shelf "species-likeness" classifier for stylized low-poly silhouettes was found. A learned embedding would need training on the game's own renders and would inherit CNN local-feature bias unless it is trained on blurred silhouettes.
